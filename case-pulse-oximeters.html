1 · The device and its rule

A Class II device with a 510(k) path

Pulse oximeters sit under one regulation in FDA's cardiovascular panel. New ones reach the US market through a 510(k), by showing they are substantially equivalent to a device already cleared.

An oximeter is a device used to transmit radiation at a known wavelength(s) through blood and to measure the blood oxygen saturation based on the amount of reflected or scattered radiation.21 CFR 870.2700(a) · Class II (performance standards)

7 · Regulatory roadmap

The path to a US clearance

A pulse oximeter is a Class II device cleared through a traditional 510(k). The work that decides the schedule is the clinical accuracy study and, for connected devices, cybersecurity.

Confirm the classification and pathwayProduct code DQA, 21 CFR 870.2700, Class II with special controls through performance standards. The device is not 510(k)-exempt, so a premarket notification under 21 CFR 807 Subpart E is required. Decide early whether it is prescription or over-the-counter and whether it is meant for spot checks or continuous monitoring: both change the testing FDA expects and the labelling.

Choose and vet predicatesPick a cleared oximeter with the same intended use and the same sensing method: transmittance (finger, ear) and reflectance (wrist, forehead) are tested to different accuracy expectations. Check every candidate's recalls and adverse-event reports before relying on it; a predicate with a recall for the component you share invites questions.

Run the clinical accuracy study firstA controlled desaturation study in healthy adults compares the device's SpO₂ with arterial blood measured by CO-oximetry, across 70 to 100 % saturation, and reports the root-mean-square accuracy (Arms) to ISO 80601-2-61. FDA's 2013 pulse oximeter guidance expects Arms of 3.0 % or better for transmittance sensors and 3.5 % for reflectance. FDA's January 2025 draft guidance adds larger cohorts with a spread of skin pigmentation, measured objectively rather than by self-report, and a performance comparison across skin tones. Book the study lab early: it is usually the longest lead time.

Assemble the rest of the evidenceBench, safety, software and human-factors evidence, listed in the evidence plan below. Each item answers a recognised consensus standard or an FDA guidance, which shortens review.

Quality system and risk fileDesign controls, supplier controls and complaint handling under the Quality Management System Regulation (21 CFR 820, in force since 2 February 2026), which incorporates ISO 13485:2016. The risk file follows ISO 14971:2019 and should draw its hazards from real field data such as the recalls and adverse events above.

Submit through eSTAR510(k)s are submitted electronically in the eSTAR template, which checks completeness up front. Content follows 21 CFR 807.87: device description, substantial-equivalence comparison, performance data and proposed labelling.

Plan for the real review clockFDA's performance goal is a decision within 90 FDA review days, but the clock stops while you answer an Additional Information request, which most oximeter submissions receive. Calendar time is what matters for launch: the recent median for this product code is 187 days (2026 decisions).

After clearanceRegister the establishment and list the device (21 CFR 807 Subpart B), label with a UDI and submit the device record to GUDID (21 CFR 830), report adverse events under 21 CFR 803 and corrections or removals under 21 CFR 806. A design or labelling change that could affect safety or effectiveness may need a new 510(k).

Outside the US the same evidence largely carries over: under the EU MDR, Annex VIII Rule 10 places oximeters in Class IIa, or IIb when they monitor vital parameters in situations where a change could mean immediate danger, which brings in a notified body.

9 · How this was made

Sources and limits

FDA figures come from the official 510(k), recall and MAUDE files (openFDA), device identifiers from AccessGUDID, and regulation text from the eCFR, refreshed as FDA publishes. Patent classes are from the Cooperative Patent Classification scheme. Counts depend on how FDA codes each record; a device coded under another product code is not counted here.
